You return to a puzzle and recognize the board, but not the decision you were about to make. Was that number certain, or just a possibility? Were you trying to clear a line, or leave room for another piece?

A short note can give you a place to begin again. The useful part is not a history of every move. It is a clear separation between what you know, what remains uncertain, and what you intended to check next.

This guide is about remembering your reasoning. A note does not save a game, restore a missing board, or establish that a move is correct.

## The Return Checklist

- **Before leaving:** check the game's own saving instructions. Do not assume a note or bookmark preserves the board.
- **Record one certain step:** write what you checked, not only the answer you liked.
- **Name the open question:** keep a candidate or planned move separate from a confirmed placement.
- **Choose one next check:** make it specific enough to repeat without reconstructing your whole session.
- **When you return:** verify the puzzle and position before trusting the note. If they differ, set the old note aside.

There is no need to turn a break into homework. A sentence or two is enough if it points to the next decision. If there is nothing useful to record, it is also fine to stop and approach the puzzle afresh later.

## Check Saving First

Before spending time on a return note, establish whether you expect to resume this particular position. Our [browser game save checklist](/blog/browser-game-save-progress-checklist/) covers that separate question, including why a saved score is not proof of a saved board.

Do not reload a valued attempt just to test whether your note matches a saved state. Follow the game's instructions, and avoid treating a screenshot, bookmark, or written description as a restorable save.

If the next visit will involve a different puzzle, record a general habit instead: "Check the whole piece before placing it" is useful without the old board. A reference to one empty square may not be.

## Leave Three Small Pieces Of Information

Use paper or a note you keep yourself. NewGames.ai does not collect or store these notes.

**Certain:** What did you establish, and why? "Checked the row, column, and box" is more informative than "this looks right." Do not label a guess as certain just to simplify the note.

**Unresolved:** What have you not established? In a number puzzle, this might be two remaining candidates. In a block puzzle, it might be whether the next visible piece fits after a clear.

**Next:** Which check would help? Choose an action such as inspecting one column or tracing a piece's full footprint. "Solve the rest" is a goal, not a starting instruction.

You can also record the official game address and a puzzle identifier if the game displays one. Use only information you can actually see; do not invent a level number or assume that today's board will be the same tomorrow.

## An Example Of A Useful Note

Here is a fictional note about a number puzzle, not a supplied board or a claim that either candidate is valid:

> Certain: I have checked the target cell's row, but not its column or box. Unresolved: I wrote down 3 and 7 as possibilities, not placed answers. Next: check that column, then the box, before entering either digit.

The note deliberately stops short of a solution. Returning to it means repeating the checks, not choosing between 3 and 7 from memory. For a worked version of that reasoning, try [finding a certain Sudoku move](/blog/how-to-find-your-first-sudoku-move/).

A block-puzzle note could be shorter: "Before placing this piece, picture the cleared board and check whether the visible square still fits." That is a question to investigate, not a promise that a square will appear next. The [fit, clear, space exercises](/blog/block-puzzle-fit-clear-leave-space/) offer small examples of this distinction.

## Check The Position Before Using The Note

When you come back, first confirm that you are looking at the same puzzle and position. Compare the relevant clues or occupied spaces with what the note describes. If another move has happened, a line has cleared, or a new puzzle has opened, the old reasoning may no longer apply.

Recheck even a familiar position before committing a move. A note can contain a copying error or an assumption you did not notice at the time. If the checks disagree, pause rather than forcing the remembered answer into place.

After a justified move, revise or cross out the outdated question. Keeping a long list of stale possibilities is less useful than leaving one clear next step.

## Make The Next Visit Optional

The point is to make returning easier, not to create an obligation to finish every puzzle. You can leave a short note, choose a natural stopping point, or decide that a different activity suits your next break.
