A block can fit on a board and still leave an awkward position behind. Before committing to a space, ask three separate questions: does the whole piece fit, what will clear, and what can fit afterward?

Try that routine with the small boards below. Each exercise isolates one decision, so you can explain your choice without planning a whole game. This is a practice guide, not a high-score formula or a claim that one placement habit works in every block puzzle.

## The Rules For These Examples

These are original NewGames.ai teaching boards, not screenshots, levels, or solutions from Blockzzle or another game. Their small 4-by-4 size keeps the choices readable. A game's actual board, pieces, and clearing rules may differ; check its own instructions before applying the examples.

For these exercises only:

- Columns are A through D, from left to right. Rows are 1 through 4, from top to bottom. **C2** means column C, row 2.
- **X** marks an occupied cell. A **dot** marks an empty cell. The symbols carry the meaning even without color.
- Place the entire piece inside the board on empty cells. Do not rotate it or overlap an X.
- After each placement, all full rows and columns clear together. Other occupied cells stay where they are; nothing falls into the gaps.
- In the last exercise, place the two supplied pieces in the stated order. There are no unknown future pieces to predict.

Each board shows the position before a move. Compare the two written options, then open the explanation. You do not need to drag anything or enter a score.

## Three Decisions To Practice

### Fit Before Planning A Clear

It is easy to focus on an inviting empty cell and overlook the rest of a piece. Trace the complete footprint first. A horizontal two-cell piece needs two adjacent empty cells in the same row; two separate gaps are not enough.

### Count The Lines Before Removing Them

Look both across and down after imagining the placement. For the rules here, you identify all completed lines on that same position before removing any cells. Otherwise, removing a row first could make you overlook a column that was also complete.

{{< block-check id="clear" >}}

### Check The Board After The Clear

An empty-cell count does not tell you which shapes will fit. Four scattered gaps cannot hold a 2-by-2 square. When you know the next piece, imagine the cleared board and look for its whole footprint again.

## A Small Checklist For Your Next Session

Use **fit, clear, space** as three separate passes:

1. **Fit:** check every cell the piece would cover, including the far end of a long shape.
2. **Clear:** identify the lines the move completes under the game's rules. Do not assume a nearly full line will disappear.
3. **Space:** picture the board after those clears. If more pieces are visible, check whether their shapes still have a place.

This routine is a way to inspect a move, not proof that it is the best possible move. A short-term clear can have a different consequence on another board, and a game may introduce rules that are not part of these exercises. Avoid turning "always clear a line" or "always use a corner" into a substitute for checking the actual position.

If you cannot yet picture the result, go back to one question at a time. First confirm that the proposed placement is legal. Then identify the completed lines. Only then inspect the spaces left behind.

## When You Come Back

These three exercises stay the same. There is no daily rotation, saved attempt history, or timed challenge. On another visit, close the explanations and describe why the other option fails, rather than trying to recall the letter of the answer.

The third example is especially useful for that check: can you explain why eight empty cells still do not provide space for the square? That distinction between total space and usable space is the main lesson to take away.
